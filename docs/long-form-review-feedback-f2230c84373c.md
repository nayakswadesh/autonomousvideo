## Point 2 — text placement on ordinary image shots

**Verified in the current Sun cut:** all 19 image-based camera shots route through the same photo renderer, which places up to two labels in small, fixed upper-left boxes. In the visual plan, 16 of 17 generated-image directions reserve the left side for overlays and one reserves the upper region. Thus the repetition originates in both the image-composition choices and the renderer: placement is not being chosen independently for each scene. The existing `overlay_safe_region` helps image generation reserve space but is not consumed by the photo renderer as a placement rule. The two authentic-image shots also use the same fixed label treatment.

**Assessment:** the user's observation is correct. A consistent visual language is useful, and upper-left labels are sometimes appropriate; the problem is that every image beat receives the same small, detached treatment irrespective of what the image and narration need. Moving labels to random corners would add variation without improving comprehension. On-image text should identify, explain, contrast, or emphasize a particular spoken idea; some shots may need no overlay at all.

**Revised proposal for discussion (9 October 2026):** use a small library of four presentation-style text entrances over image shots, without introducing object detection, callout targeting, or a new semantic overlay contract:

1. **Fade and rise:** text fades in while moving a short distance upward. Calm default for ordinary phrases.
2. **Wipe reveal:** a short accent rule sweeps in, revealing a concise phrase from one side. Useful for a short label.
3. **Slide-in plate:** a compact, locally protected text panel enters from the side, settles, then remains still. Useful for a longer one- or two-line statement over a busy image.
4. **Gentle emphasis pop:** a short question or number grows slightly into place and settles without bounce. Use sparingly, not on every label.

These draw on [PowerPoint's documented Fade, Wipe, Fly In, and Zoom effects](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/animation-effects-available-in-powerpoint-for-the-web). Microsoft also [advises restraint rather than stacking many styles in one presentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-gb/powerpoint/training/video-animations-and-transitions-for-powerpoint-web); four effects may be available in the library, but one or two should dominate a given chapter, with the others reserved for emphasis. The timings and exact look are design hypotheses to review, not claims of proven retention gains.

**Generic selection:** keep the approved overlay words and measured speech cues. Choose eligible effects from measurable text properties: ordinary phrase -> fade/rise; short label -> wipe; longer text or busy background -> slide-in plate; short question (punctuation) or number -> pop. Rotate among eligible effects to avoid adjacent repetition, but never choose an effect that makes the text unreadable. Respect existing image safe-region guidance; within it, allow a few layout positions instead of always the same upper-left box. For authentic images without such guidance, use a conservative protected text zone. No project-specific words or topic rules are hard-coded, and this proposal requires no new model call.

**Timing and safeguards:** trigger the entrance at the related spoken phrase, complete it quickly (roughly 0.3–0.6 seconds), then hold long enough to read. Do not animate by letter, repeatedly pulse text, or move it after the entrance. Keep typography code-rendered. Check text fit, caption collisions, focal-subject occlusion, and local contrast on the moving frames; use local protection only where necessary, not a whole-image darkening pass. [Material Design's imagery guidance](https://m1.material.io/style/imagery.html) recommends targeted text protection, and [W3C's contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum) gives useful readability targets.

**Trial:** render the *same approved text, images, narration, and cue times* from several Sun shots with these four effects, plus the unchanged baseline. Review a short motion reel and phone-size stills. This tests whether the effects draw attention without distracting; then run the identical rendering code on unrelated image shots to check generality. The former callout/subject-localization proposal is superseded for point 2.

This remains a design proposal, **not yet a settled point-2 solution**. Image/camera motion (point 4) and image-generation specificity (point 8) will be reviewed separately.

## Point 4 — repeated image camera movement

**Verified in the current Sun cut:** all 17 generated-image camera shots have identical motion parameters: `focus_start=(0.46, 0.43)`, `focus_end=(0.54, 0.41)`, and zoom `1.04 -> 1.15`. The crop center therefore travels toward the right and slightly upward while zooming in; the *pictured content* can appear to drift in the opposite direction, so “left-to-right” is not a precise description of the camera math. The two authentic-image shots use `contain` presentation with zoom `1.00 -> 1.04`; in this renderer, `contain` ignores focus_start/end, so they scale in place rather than pan. The sameness is encoded in `workflow/motion_planning.py` and executed by `rendering/longform_motion.py`; it is not a choice made per image by the director.

**Assessment:** the user's perception of repetitive motion is well founded. Simply reversing direction every other shot would be cosmetic and could crop a subject or push attention away from the spoken idea. [Apple's Final Cut Pro documentation](https://support.apple.com/en-lamr/guide/final-cut-pro/verb8e5de9c/mac) describes Ken Burns movement as independently chosen start/end crop positions and sizes, including zoom in or out; it does not imply that more motion variety automatically improves retention.

**Narrow proposal for discussion:** give image shots a small, reusable motion vocabulary: (a) slow centered push-in, (b) slow pull-back/reveal, (c) gentle lateral or vertical drift when the image has enough overscan and its subject stays in frame, and (d) near-still hold followed by a modest push. Do not force a move where an authentic photograph, low-resolution image, or tightly composed subject would be damaged by cropping. The choice should respect image dimensions, `fill` versus `contain`, crop safety, shot duration, and adjacent-shot repetition. Preserve the visual subject and any area needed for point 2 text; validate start, midpoint, and end frames. The text entrance and image movement should be coordinated but chosen independently.

This is a **point-4 analysis and candidate direction**. No code or live project has been changed.

The plan, not a render-time random choice, determines the result. Keep the image and text choices separate: point 2 controls how a label enters; point 4 controls the underlying image.

### Point 6 implementation plan — visual planner owns the screen (saved; local implementation below)

**Decision and boundary:** the visual planner is the sole creative owner of *what appears on screen*. ### Point 9 clarification — expand explanatory capability, not merely variety

The user clarified that point 9 primarily asks **which missing treatments would help the viewer understand ideas that the current registry cannot depict well**. The preceding frequency analysis explains where to look, but increasing treatment diversity is not the objective. A new treatment is justified by a distinct spoken relationship, an intelligible visual mechanism, and a case where current treatments either omit that relationship or distort it.

The most credible gaps in the present registry are: (1) `evidence/signal_to_inference`: one measured clue visibly supports an inference; current evidence treatments assume competitors or prediction-versus-observation. Treat `process/state_progression` as a possible refinement of existing `process/transformation`, not an automatic new family; the registry already has comparable bars, before/after, timelines, and maps. The exact family-versus-treatment boundaries need prototype review, not a naming exercise.

## Point 10 — does the recap earn its runtime?

**What the Sun cut actually contains:** Chapter 5 is not wholly a recap. Its first 32.8 seconds deliver the necessary story payoff: the Sun sheds outer material and leaves a white dwarf rather than exploding as a supernova, with model-dependent timing and detail. The next five beats, from 32.8 to 68.2 seconds within that chapter, are a 35.4-second recap/synthesis. They return in order to meteorite evidence, present-day fusion/neutrinos, modeled red-giant and white-dwarf future, the distinction between kinds of knowledge, and the scope of the Sun's endpoint. The final 18.8 seconds are an audience question and calls to action, a separate issue for points 11–12. The full review video is about 5:43.5, so the recap alone takes approximately 10.3% of the cut.

**Editorial assessment:** the concern is substantially valid. Re-listing meteorites, fusion, and future stages tells the viewer mostly what they have just heard, while the story's principal answer has already arrived. The fourth recap beat contributes a genuine synthesis—past reconstructed from early solids, present tested through observations, future projected through models—and the fifth prevents an overbroad implication that the Sun's stellar endpoint ends the Solar System or universe. Those insights are worth considering in the ending, but do not obviously require five beats and 35 seconds. The recap's first two shots visibly return to earlier subject matter; the later synthesis is displayed as a statement card. We cannot infer actual viewer drop-off from the cut alone.

**Direction to discuss, not an approved implementation plan:** keep the white-dwarf payoff. Do not require a standalone recap segment in every long-form script. Prefer a brief concluding *insight or callback* only when it completes the opening question, resolves a possible misconception, or changes how the viewer understands the story; avoid a chronological inventory of earlier chapters. For this Sun story, a concise distinction among reconstructed past, observed present, and modeled future could preserve the distinctive insight, perhaps with the scope clarification folded into it. The exact wording and runtime must be developed during the later narration discussion, not silently edited in the approved cut. Points 11–12 will consider the separate audience invitation/CTA.

**How to judge it after publication:** YouTube does not prescribe a blanket no-recap rule. Its [audience-retention guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en) recommends examining per-video dips, spikes, and top moments; that report would reveal whether a particular closing recap is skipped or watched. This is a proposed editorial direction, not a measured retention result or a production change. Point 9's registry addition is implemented; autonomous selection on a new live topic is not yet measured.

## Point 11 — does the closing question actually invite comments?

**Assessment:** the user's observation is correct.
